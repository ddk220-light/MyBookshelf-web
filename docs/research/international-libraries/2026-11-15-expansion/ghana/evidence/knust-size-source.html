KNUST Library Trains Medical Residents in Research Skills and Ethical Publishing

The Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology (KNUST) Library System has organised a training workshop for medical residents to strengthen their research skills, enhance their use of digital research tools and promote ethical scholarly publishing. The programme was designed to equip participants with the knowledge and practical skills needed to navigate the university’s research environment, produce credible scholarly work and make informed decisions about academic publishing. Leading the training, the University Librarian, Dr. Richard Bruce Lamptey, said the initiative forms part of the Library System’s commitment to supporting medical professionals and advancing the University’s research and innovation agenda. He emphasised the importance of equipping medical staff with the requisite skills, tools and guidance to establish themselves as researchers and contribute meaningfully to the University’s research output. “Medical staff need the right skills, tools and guidance to establish themselves as researchers and contribute meaningfully to the University’s research output. Through this training, we seek to provide them with the knowledge they need to conduct and publish research responsibly while increasing the visibility and impact of their work,” he said. Participants, with guidance from senior library staff, received practical instruction on creating and managing academic profiles on platforms such as ORCID, Google Scholar, ResearchGate and Scopus. These platforms support researchers in showcasing their scholarly contributions, improving the discoverability of their publications and strengthening their academic profiles. Dr. Lamptey noted that effective management of researchers’ online profiles could expand the reach of their scholarly work and enhance their contribution to global research and scholarship. The training also highlighted the importance of research integrity and responsible publishing. Participants were introduced to the appropriate use of Turnitin, the interpretation of similarity reports and ways of identifying predatory journals. “Research integrity is fundamental to credible scholarship. You must understand how to interpret similarity reports appropriately and ensure that your work meets the required standards of originality and ethical publication,” Dr. Lamptey stressed. Additionally, the sessions addressed the University’s requirements for verifying scholarly publications for academic promotion. Participants were also guided on accessing the Library System’s scholarly databases and electronic resources to support their research activities. The workshop underscores the KNUST Library System’s commitment to strengthening research capacity, promoting responsible scholarship and empowering the University community with the digital and information skills needed to excel in teaching, learning and research. Science Friday, Episode 10: Beyond the Bite: Insects, Public Health, and Sustainable Solutions

A researcher at the Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology, Kumasi (KNUST), has advocated the use of insects to manage organic waste and improve soil fertility as part of sustainable approaches to environmental management. Dr. Sandra Abankwa Kwarteng, a Senior Lecturer at the Department of Theoretical and Applied Biology, urged a better understanding of insects to harness their benefits while controlling those that threaten public health. She was speaking at the 10th edition of the KNUST Library System’s Science Friday initiative. “In as much as it is true that they are problems, they can also be used as a solution. And in actual fact, as a sustainable solution,” she said. Dr. Kwarteng highlighted the potential of insect-based approaches to waste management and soil improvement, calling for evidence-based interventions that benefit both human health and the environment. She stressed that effective insect control requires identifying species and understanding their habitats and behaviour before choosing control measures. “It is so crucial that we don't just think about the bites. There is a whole system associated with the bites,” she said. She cautioned against treating all insects alike, noting that different species thrive under different environmental conditions. “If you don't know these two, you have no business controlling them. You can't. You just waste money and resources,” she said. Dr. Kwarteng also urged researchers and public health practitioners to assess community knowledge, attitudes and practices before introducing interventions. This should include understanding activities that could increase or reduce insect populations. “You want to really understand what they know about that particular insect,” she said. The University Librarian, Dr. Richard Bruce Lamptey, described the discussion as timely, citing the diverse roles insects play in public health and the environment. While some insects transmit diseases, others support biodiversity, food security and ecological balance, he said. He called for a more informed understanding of insects to guide interventions that protect public health while preserving the environment. Dr. Lamptey also reaffirmed the role of university libraries as centres of knowledge creation, discovery, research and lifelong learning. He said initiatives such as Science Friday provide opportunities for experts to share knowledge and for the university community to engage with scientific issues affecting society.
